How this was built, and what it can't tell you

Posts are collected from the public LinkedIn activity of the tracked profiles listed below. Each post is scored as reactions + (__W_COMMENTS__ × comments) + (__W_SHARES__ × shares), on the basis that a comment or a share costs the reader more than a reaction and is the better signal that a topic has landed.

Named subjects

The default board works at the level of specific things — a person, a brand, a piece of work, an event. These are pulled from three places: LinkedIn's own @-mentions, which come typed as a person or a company and are the most reliable; hashtags; and runs of capitalised words in the post body and any attached article title.

That third source is the useful one and the noisy one. It is filtered to drop sentence-opening capitals, job titles, credit lists, bare first names and generic acronyms. Some noise still gets through, so read a row before you act on it rather than trusting the label. Places and acronyms are typed and hidden by default, because they are usually context rather than subject.

Subjects named in only one post are kept, so the tags under each post are complete, but the two-voice threshold keeps them out of the overlap board. Switching to Any shows them and will surface considerably more noise.

LinkedIn itself is excluded. It gets named constantly and tells you nothing about what the conversation is about. The exclusion list is the right place for anything else that is self-referential rather than a subject.

Names are folded where they are obviously the same subject, so a surname on its own merges into the full name it appears with. This is a text-matching exercise, not entity resolution: two people with the same surname will be merged wrongly, and the same brand written two ways may stay split.

Broad themes

The second board mode uses a keyword framework over the same text. A post can sit in up to four themes, and roughly a third match none — general motivational content with no subject matter falls out, which is intended. Themes are good for the shape of the conversation and too general to brief a post against on their own.

Read the overlap, not the volume

Rows are ranked by the number of distinct voices first, engagement second. That ordering is deliberate: engagement totals are dominated by whoever posts most and has the largest following, whereas the count of separate voices is a much steadier signal that a subject is genuinely live in this community rather than one person's hobby horse.

Two voices is a low bar on a watchlist of eleven, and on a short period it can be coincidence. Treat two as worth a look and three or more as worth acting on.

Uneven history per voice

The scraper returns a fixed number of recent posts per profile, so a prolific poster's history reaches back days while a light poster's reaches back months. Any period longer than the shortest history below will understate the voices marked in amber. For a like-for-like comparison, stay inside the shortest window.

Other limits worth stating to a client

  • Engagement counts are as at the collection date; recent posts are still accruing and will be understated against older ones.
  • Reposts are attributed to the tracked voice that shared them, with the original author noted.
  • Neither board catches intent. A row tells you a subject came up, not whether the voices were agreeing, arguing or taking the opposite side.
  • A subject can be named in passing. The post text is on the page so you can check whether it was the point of the post or a footnote.
  • This is a snapshot, not a live feed. The collection date is stamped at the top.
